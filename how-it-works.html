Aegida Privacy policy

A quiet helper that looks out for scams on your phone

Aegida (say it AY-gih-dah) is a free Android app. It looks at the notices on your phone and tells you, in plain words, when one looks like a scam. Only if you say so, it can also send a short note to one person you trust.

If a family member or a friend sent you this page, it is because they care about you.

How it works

  1. 1. Set it up In about five minutes. You give your first name and pick one person you trust, called your guardian. The words are big, and someone can sit with you the first time.
  2. 2. It looks out quietly You do not need to open it each day. If a message has a link that looks like a scam, or a website sends you fake warnings, Aegida tells you in plain words and says what to do.
  3. 3. You stay in charge Telling your guardian is your choice, and you can switch it off any time. Nothing is sent to anyone until you say yes.

What you will see

A small notice on your screen says "Aegida is looking out for you", so you can see it is running. You can swipe it away.

Someone you trust, only if you say yes

  • The note never has the words of your messages or a whole web address. It can name the app a message came from, and show a website with most of its letters hidden, like pa*****.example.
  • The first note they get says: "Aegida is set up on Ruth's phone. You will get a message here if it needs help."
  • They need only the free ntfy app. They cannot see your phone, and they cannot change anything on it.
  • You can pick a different person, or turn this off, whenever you like.

What Aegida will not do

  • It does not keep or send the words in your messages. It only looks for scam links, right on your phone.
  • It never opens a link from a message.
  • It does not look at your photos, contacts, location, microphone or camera, and it does not listen to calls.
  • It has no ads, no account to make and no tracking.
It cannot catch every scam. When it says "No known problems found", it means it found none that it knows about. If something feels wrong, stop and call someone you trust.

Three habits that help, with or without Aegida

  1. Slow down. Scammers want you to hurry, so you do not have time to think.
  2. Be careful when someone you do not know contacts you. A real bank or government office will never ask you to pay with gift cards.
  3. Talk to someone you trust before you do anything.

These habits follow the advice of the US Federal Trade Commission for older adults.

Questions

Does it cost anything?

No. Aegida is free, with no ads and no account.

Who can see what is on my phone?

Nobody at Aegida. What Aegida looks at stays on your phone. The only thing Aegida sends is the short note to your guardian, and only if you turned that on. If you also chose the website-blocking step in setup, your phone asks Cloudflare, a large internet company, which websites to refuse. That is a phone setting, and you can turn it off.

What is the website-blocking step in setup?

It is optional. It sets one switch on your phone, called Private DNS, so that your phone refuses to open websites that are known to be scams. The blocking is done by Cloudflare, not by Aegida. Aegida only checks that the switch is still set and tells you, and your guardian if you chose that, if it gets turned off. You can skip it and Aegida still checks your messages and apps.

What does the person I choose get, exactly?

One short note when something serious happens, such as the example above. They also get a note if Aegida has not been able to check in for two days, because the phone may be off. They do not get your messages or any whole web address. A note about a scam message can say which app it came from and show the website with most of its letters hidden, like pa*****.example, so you can tell sites apart.

What does my guardian need to do?

Install the free ntfy app, tap the plus button and type the name from your invitation email. That is all, and no account is needed. They should save the email: the same name sets up another phone or a computer later. On a computer they can open the web address in the email, which shows the notes while that page is open.

Can I turn it off, or change the person?

Yes, both, in My settings. If you pick someone else, the first person is told once, and their old link stops working.

What if it says something is wrong and I am not sure?

Do not tap the link. Slow down and call someone you trust. The "Call a guardian" button dials the number you gave.

Will it work on my phone, and do I need the internet?

Aegida works on Android phones with Android 10 or newer. It is not for iPhones. It checks on your phone, and uses the internet only to update its list of known scams and to send your guardian's note.

Aegida is in testing with a few families right now. If someone sent you this page, ask them to help you install it.

How Aegida (AY-gih-dah) works, in detail

For readers who want to check the mechanism. This page describes Aegida 0.3.6 and was checked against the app's code. Where something has not been tested on a real device, it says so.

In two lines

Aegida reads the title and text of notifications through Android's notification-access service, checks them on the phone against fixed rules and a signed list of known bad hosts, and tells the owner in plain words. If the owner turned it on, it sends one fixed sentence to one chosen person through ntfy.sh.

What happens to a notification

  1. Read. Android's notification-access service gives Aegida the title and text of every notification another app posts. Each part is cut at 4,096 characters, so a link beyond that is not seen. Aegida's own notifications are skipped.
  2. Check. Nine detectors run on every check, one after another, so two triggers cannot overlap. Checks run when a notification arrives, when the listener reconnects, whenever the app is opened, and from a daily background job (every 24 hours, with up to 6 hours of flexibility).
  3. Record. A finding keeps the host, package, channel, app label and notification key. For a scam message or a website pop-up it also keeps who it was from, the first 200 characters of what it said and when, so the warning can say what it was. That is cleared when the owner taps "Got it". History keeps a short line about each alert for 90 days (the app, the sender, a few words with any link taken out, or the website name). None of it is ever sent.
  4. Tell. The owner sees a banner and the Home screen. Only website pop-ups are hidden; a message with a scam link is left in place, because it may come from family.
  5. Optionally tell one person. See "What leaves the phone" below.

Link shorteners are recognized but do not make a finding on their own. A link that matches nothing makes no finding.

The scam lists

  • Format. A file of sorted 8-byte values. A host is hashed as the host, a link as host plus path without a trailing slash, an app as pkg: plus its lower-case package name, and a certificate as cert: plus its lower-case SHA-1. A lookup is a binary search. A match shows that something matched, not which entry. The live file is about 9 MB.
  • Rules before it replaces the old lists. At least 1,000 host and link entries; no more than 100,000 package or certificate entries; not dated more than an hour ahead; strictly newer than the stored lists; host and link count at least half of the stored count; and the file is rejected over 32 MiB. The new file is written to a temporary file and renamed, so a failure never leaves half a list.
  • Left out on purpose. About 45 shared hosts (such as github.io and bit.ly) are dropped from the host list so one bad page does not block a whole site; those can match only by exact link.
  • Staleness. "Lists old" means the file's own build date is more than three days old, not the last download. Home then says it could not check, instead of "No known problems found".

WhatTo whomWhenContents

For scam messages and website pop-ups the sentence can also name the app and show the web address as its first two letters, stars and its ending (pa*****.example). Invitation emailThe owner's own email appOnce in setup, or when the owner taps "Send the invitation again"The ntfy install links and the private topic link. The owner presses Send; Aegida cannot tell whether it was sent.

Aegida sends nothing else. The only thing that comes in is the scam lists. The one other thing the phone itself sends, if the owner chose the optional website-blocking step, is covered under "Website blocking" below.

  • The topic is the password. A topic is aegida_ plus 40 random letters and digits from a cryptographically strong random generator, about 238 bits, made on the phone. There is no account, token or API key. Anyone who has the link could read the notes or send a false one, which is why the invitation says to keep it private, and why changing the guardian makes a new topic.
  • "Hasn't checked in". After every daily check Aegida re-arms one delayed message (two days) under the same sequence id, which replaces the waiting one. If the phone stays silent for about two days, ntfy delivers it. Turning alerts off cancels it.
  • Repeats. For non-urgent kinds, the guardian is told again only after 24 hours, then 48, 96, and 7 days, if the same kind keeps coming back. Urgent kinds are never held back. A warning still open after a day gets one reminder. After a guardian change, earlier history does not count for the new guardian.
  • Offline. A note that cannot be sent is queued and retried after every check (15 minutes, doubling, up to 6 hours), and given up after 7 days, which History says. Messages for a replaced guardian are dropped.
  • Never leaves the phone. The text of notifications, contacts, texts, calls, location, microphone, camera, the list of installed apps, and the findings. There is no analytics, crash-reporting or advertising code: the app's libraries are Jetpack Compose, Hilt, Room, WorkManager, OkHttp and kotlinx.serialization.
  • Stored on the phone. The owner's name and picture, the guardian's name, email and phone number, the topic, a baseline of the phone from when setup ended, findings, 90 days of history, the queue, and the downloaded lists, in the app's private storage. It is excluded from cloud backup and phone-to-phone transfer. It is not encrypted by Aegida beyond what Android itself does for app storage.

Setting up the guardian's devices

The invitation email asks the guardian to install ntfy, tap + and type the topic name (on Android the link ntfy://ntfy.sh/<topic> does the same once ntfy is installed). The topic is the only thing needed, so any phone or computer that subscribes to it gets the same notes; the email suggests saving it for that reason. The https://ntfy.sh/<topic> web page works on a computer, but it shows notes only while it is open, so it is a second view and not a replacement for the phone app.

Optional: an ntfy account. A guardian who wants to keep the same subscriptions across devices can create a free ntfy account at ntfy.sh (an email address is optional at sign-up) and log in on each device. ntfy describes syncing between devices for logged-in users, but how much it syncs depends on the app, so if a device does not show the notes, add the topic name there too. Aegida does not need an account and never asks for one. An account is between the guardian and ntfy: it lets ntfy connect the topic to that person.

Website blocking (optional, a phone setting)

Setup offers to set the phone's Private DNS to security.cloudflare-dns.com, Cloudflare's malware-blocking resolver. The owner types it into the phone's own settings; Aegida only copies the address and opens the page. Aegida reads the setting and nothing more: it does not see the sites visited. Once set, the phone (not Aegida) sends each website name it looks up to Cloudflare over an encrypted connection, and Cloudflare refuses names on its malware list. Aegida cannot see or change that traffic. If the setting is later changed, Aegida says "Website protection is off" and offers the steps again. The owner can leave the step out or turn the setting off at any time; My settings shows whether it is On or Off.

What comes in

The daily job asks wizoi.github.io (GitHub Pages) for two files, feed.sig and then feed.bin, with a plain GET. The request names the file and nothing else: no identifiers, nothing about the phone or the owner. GitHub, like any website, can see the internet address that asked.

What it cannot do

  • It is not a scan of the whole phone. It reads notification titles and text only. A scam text that never makes a notification, or a messaging app that hides previews, is not seen. Android 15 and newer hides the content of one-time-code notifications from apps like this.
  • It cannot tell that a link is fine. It matches known lists and the rules above. No match means "No known problems found", which is not a promise about everything.
  • New-app and spying-app checks are limited. They cover the lists, plus "installed from outside Google Play" for apps that arrive after setup. A new app is noticed at the next check (a notification, opening the app, or the daily job), not the moment it is installed.
  • It cannot change settings for the owner. Android needs the owner's tap. The owner can uninstall Aegida or remove its access; the guardian then hears if alerts are on and the notification-access warning is raised, or after about two days of silence.
  • It cannot know the invitation arrived. Aegida cannot tell whether the email was sent or the guardian subscribed in ntfy.
  • Samsung phones. Samsung's Auto Blocker cannot be read by a Google Play app, so Aegida takes the owner's word. Samsung may put an unopened app to sleep after about three days; this has not yet been tested on a real phone.
  • ntfy.sh is a free public service with no promise about uptime. If it is down or limits traffic, notes are queued and retried.

Rules the build enforces

  • Network access goes through one client to two fixed hosts. There is no way to fetch an address taken from a message or notification.
  • Banned in the build: an accessibility service, a VPN service, overlays, full-screen alerts, a battery-exemption request, SMS and audio recording. A new permission needs an explicit decision.
  • No secrets in the app. The scam-list signing key is held only as a GitHub secret; the app contains only the public key.
  • Every sentence the owner or guardian reads lives in one place, and automated checks reject words that promise too much and all-capital labels.
